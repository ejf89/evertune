Part one

What we built

Evertune's system can already talk to one AI provider. This project adds a second one — Google's Gemini model — without changing or removing anything that already worked.

The harder part of the assignment wasn't connecting to Gemini — it was proving that connection would hold up once real traffic hit it. That took three separate rounds of testing before we'd sign off on it.

Part two

What we found

Four things worth knowing before relying on this model at scale — each one is something Evertune specifically needs to account for, not a generic "AI is complicated" caveat.

1. This AI model has a hidden cost most people wouldn't expect

Before Gemini writes the answer you actually see, it does a round of internal "thinking" — and Google charges for that thinking, even though you never see it. At normal settings, most of what you're paying for is invisible.

Important: turning "thinking" up does not give you a bigger or better answer — it's reasoning that happens before writing, not extra effort spent on what you actually read. In our testing, the highest thinking setting produced a slightly shorter visible answer than the normal setting, while costing more. It's a dial that trades money and time for invisible reasoning — not a quality upgrade we've actually verified.

What this means for Evertune: the "thinking" setting is the single biggest cost lever on this model. It's tunable — but someone needs to decide how much answer quality is worth paying for, because leaving it on default isn't a neutral choice.

2. It can send back nothing — and still charge full price

We deliberately tested an edge case: limit how long the answer can be, while thinking is turned way up. The model spent its whole budget thinking and had nothing left to write down.

9 / 12test requests came back
completely blank

No error was raised. The system said "success" and billed in full for an empty answer.

What this means for Evertune: anything that counts brand mentions from this model needs to specifically check for this case, or it will quietly undercount — and not randomly. It happens more on harder questions, which biases exactly the data you'd care about most.

3. "Give me a consistent answer" doesn't fully mean that

There's a setting meant to make the model deterministic — the same question should get the same answer, every time. We asked one question 100 times with that setting on.

7different answers came back,
not 1

The instability wasn't spread evenly — some brands showed up consistently no matter what, others were essentially a coin flip.

What this means for Evertune: a single spot-check of this model is not reliable evidence on its own. How many times you'd need to ask the same question to trust the answer isn't one fixed number — it depends on the specific brand/category, and that can only be known by checking.

4. Under heavy load, it doesn't break — it just gets slow, with no warning

We simulated up to 2,000 people using it at the exact same moment — far beyond anything needed day to day. Not one request ever failed. But past a certain point, every response started taking noticeably longer, and that slowdown grew the more we pushed it.

1 to 2,000 at once

We also tested this from three completely separate, simultaneous processes at once, to rule out that the slowdown was just an artifact of our one test setup. The result was mixed, not a clean confirmation either way: each of the three saw roughly the speed you'd expect for its own smaller share of the load, not the full combined slowdown — but one of them also hit our first-ever error in this entire project. That's evidence a real capacity limit exists somewhere above normal usage; it's not full proof the exact number above is precise.

What this means for Evertune: a monitoring approach that only watches for errors would never notice this system straining — it degrades silently. Response speed needs to be watched directly as usage grows, not just whether requests succeed.

Bottom line

  • It works, and it's been proven under far more load than normal usage would require — up to 2,000 simultaneous requests with zero failures.
  • The real cost driver is a setting, not a surprise bill — "thinking" can be tuned once someone decides what tradeoff between cost and answer quality makes sense.
  • Two things need a process change before this scales up: a check for the "blank answer, still charged" case, and a plan for how many times to sample a question before trusting the result.
  • One number is still provisional: the load-testing was run from our own test setup, not fully independent infrastructure. The overall shape of the finding (it slows down, doesn't break) is solid; the exact capacity number should be re-verified before being used to size real infrastructure.
